# First Tick

*Reading-level experiment · target: elementary school · rewritten from `docs/posts/0000-first-tick.md` · original untouched*

---

*A note first: this post was written as a plan, before the code was finished. The story below is what we hope the program will do.*

Imagine a computer program that makes up a whole universe. Not a game you play. A universe you *read*, like a history book that writes itself.

Here is a story that universe told. Two made-up peoples share a galaxy. One, the Vessari, loves trading. They sell a gas called heliox, and they keep raising the price. The other people, the Khedrun, get tired of paying. Their leaders take a vote: five say fight, two say don't. So there is a war. Ships are lost. One place changes hands four times. After many days, both sides are too worn out to keep fighting, so the war just stops. By day one thousand, the traders have less than half the money they started with.

Here is the strange part. Nobody wrote that story. We only wrote the rules, the way you might write the rules of a board game. Then the computer played the game by itself for a thousand pretend days. That took about two seconds. The story fell out on its own.

**The magic number.** Every universe starts from one number, like a recipe number. This universe came from the number 1893. If you give the program the same number, you get the exact same universe, down to the last ship. Change one digit, and you get a universe nobody has ever seen. That means a universe fits in your pocket. You can tell a friend, "Try 1893," and they will see the very same war you saw.

**The diary.** Inside the program, nothing "happens" unless it gets written down in one big diary. Every entry says when it happened, where, and what caused it. The stories you read are all copied out of that diary. The project calls this diary "the annals." If the diary is bad, the whole thing is bad, because reading it is the whole point.

**Nobody knows everything.** In this universe, no people gets to see the truth. Each people only knows the news that has reached it, like mail. Mail can be slow, and it can arrive garbled. So two sides can disagree about their own war. Far away, there is a tiny people who has never even heard of the big empires. No mail ever reached them. This year their diary says: good harvest, and some strange lights in the sky.

**What went wrong.** We messed up fast. One tool in our programming language reads a list in whatever order it feels like — like grabbing socks from a drawer with your eyes closed. Because of that, our very first universe came out different every time we ran it. Same recipe, different cake. That is the one thing we promised would never happen. We fixed it, and we wrote down what we learned.

**Why we are doing this.** To learn, and to share what we learn. Every new piece of the program comes with a lesson about the computer ideas inside it. Two of us are building it: Mike, a human teacher who decides everything, and Claude, an AI that helps write. One rule keeps it fair: nothing counts until Mike can explain it all by himself, with the AI out of the room.

The clock is wound. Same number, same universe. See you at day two thousand.
