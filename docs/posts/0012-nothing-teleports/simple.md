# Nothing Teleports

*Post 0012 · the simple version — plain language, same facts · the
complete essay: [complete.md](./complete.md)*

*Previously: our simulated universe learned that news takes time to
travel — a nation eight days away learns about a war eight days
late. But we cheated in two places, and we wrote the cheats down:
when grain was sold, it teleported instantly into the buyer's
granary, and when raiders plundered a town, the loot appeared
instantly back home. This post is about ending those cheats.*

---

Watch a grain merchant's ledger across sixteen days of universe
1893:

```
tick   11 ← tick   11 · the vessari dispatch 12 grain to the khedrun
tick   19 ← tick   19 · 1176¢ from the khedrun reach the vessari
tick   27 ← tick   19 · 12 grain from the vessari reach the khedrun
```

Each line shows two dates: when the merchant learned a thing, and
when it happened. On day 11 they ship twelve sacks — they know
immediately, since it happened at their own gates. On day 19 the
payment arrives in their treasury — also known immediately, same
reason. But they don't find out their grain *reached the buyer*
until day 27 — eight days after it actually arrived, and a week
after they'd already been paid. For sixteen days, those sacks were
nowhere a person could point to. They were on the road. As of this
card, the road is a real place.

The change sounds small and touches everything. Selling grain now
means the sacks physically leave your granary, ride toward the
buyer for eight days, and only then land in theirs. Payment rides
the other way. Raiders who plunder a town have to carry the loot
home — a new event marks the war party's return, and only then does
the treasure enter their books. And because things can now be
*between* places, the auditor — the independent bookkeeper who
refolds all of history and checks that nothing is ever conjured or
vanished — keeps a new account for it. Real accountants have a name
for exactly this: goods in transit. At the end of a thousand
simulated days, the audit balances with 40 sacks of grain and
2,000¢ that are, at that instant, nowhere at all — except on a
road, in the books, conserved.

One distinction runs under all of it, in Mike's words: grain moving
is *net zero* — it leaves one place and arrives at another — but
information moving is a *copy*, because telling someone something
doesn't make you forget it. Matter and news ride the same roads at
the same speed; they just do opposite things to the books when they
arrive.

Then the simulation surprised us. Last card, we made a great
ceremony of nations' books drifting away from the truth: when a
trade at the distant exchange instantly moved grain out of your
granary, your own records ran days behind reality, and we built
careful machinery proving the gap always equaled exactly the news
still in transit. This card, that drift *died*. Zero gap, every
seed, every day. The reason: now that goods travel, everything that
changes your books happens at your own front gates — your shipments
leave from home, deliveries arrive at home, raiders hit your home,
your war parties return to it. You witness every change to your own
wealth in person. The old drift, it turns out, was never really
about slow news — it was a symptom of the teleporting, of things
being taken from your granary by a deal struck far away, faster
than word of it could reach you. Make matter honest, and a nation
knows its own books perfectly again — while staying just as
ignorant as ever about everyone else's.

The old absurdity died with it. Last card, raiders teleported their
loot home but the *news* of the loot traveled slowly, so the
Khedrun spent days starving next to granaries that were secretly
full. Now the party, the grain, and the news all arrive together —
that particular madness is structurally impossible. What replaced
it is honest hardship, and we test for it: somewhere in a thousand
days, a nation goes hungry while grain addressed to it is already
on the road. Relief in transit, bellies empty. Only real travel can
produce that.

We got things wrong, as usual. Our first test for that
hungry-beside-the-road story searched the trade shipments and found
nothing — a thousand days, no overlap — and only then did we notice
the story had been in the war column all along: the hungry Khedrun,
their loot-laden war parties still days from home. (The test also
had a plain bug, which failing honestly exposed.) We'd also bet
that slowing the grain down might bring back the price wars that
went extinct last card. It didn't — all twenty wars in a thousand
days are still hunger wars — and per the house rule, the toy
universe doesn't get retuned to look like its past selves. And we
almost built a fix for a problem that didn't exist: a way to make
nations aware of their own shipments instantly, before noticing the
architecture already guaranteed it.

The biggest surprise, though, was the good kind. Nothing in our
design conversations predicted the drift's death — we designed for
a small, bounded gap and got zero. The simulation has started
having consequences we don't call in advance. That's not a bug in
our foresight. That's the machine beginning to be a universe.
